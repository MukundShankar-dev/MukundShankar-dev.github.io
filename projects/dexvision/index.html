A project in progress

DexVision

I want a robot to carry out instructions like “put this part on the inspection pad and press Start” using reusable skills. An LLM should choose the object, destination, and sequence of skills—not spend tokens generating every wrist movement and finger command. The goal is to keep expensive language-model reasoning at the task level while smaller controllers handle movement and feedback.

That means building the skills first. I started by controlling a simulated Shadow Hand with a webcam, recorded demonstrations, and tried training policies to imitate them. Those policies failed. I then changed how movements were controlled and demonstrations were collected. This journal follows those experiments, including what the failures did—and did not—tell me. The LLM orchestrator and vision-based execution are still future work.

Phase 1

Making the simulated hand follow mine

The first goal was teleoperation: move my hand in front of a camera and have a simulated hand follow it. I tested camera input, tracking, finger measurements, and robot control separately so that a bad movement could be traced back to its source.

Camera first, then hand tracking

I opened the webcam with OpenCV's cv2.VideoCapture, displayed the frame rate, and checked that q closed it cleanly. The feed ran at about 30 FPS. Only then did I add hand detection.

MediaPipe Hands supplies 21 landmarks, handedness, and a confidence score. The tracker also supports the newer Tasks Hand Landmarker API, loading a local model and processing timestamped video frames. It converts OpenCV's BGR frames to RGB, draws the skeleton, and returns an explicit no-hand result when tracking is lost.

Left/right labels were initially reversed: MediaPipe assumes a selfie-mirrored image, but the webcam input was unmirrored. Correcting that convention fixed the labels without changing the underlying landmarks.

Turning landmarks into finger movement

I used geometry, not another learned model, to extract finger curl, thumb–index pinch distance, and palm orientation. Live bars showed whether an open hand, fist, or pointing gesture produced the expected controls before I connected them to the robot.

Raw measurements jittered between frames. An exponential moving average smoothed each control: s = α × x + (1 − α) × s_previous. Lower alpha reduced jitter but added lag. Tracking loss held the last stable controls, with an optional decay toward neutral, rather than sending missing values to the simulator.

Connecting the controls to the Shadow Hand

I first checked a minimal MuJoCo scene and a simple debug hand: move one joint, then play open, fist, point, pinch, and relaxed poses. I then switched to the right Shadow Hand E3M5 from MuJoCo Menagerie. Its 20 wrist/finger actuators include tendon-coupled joints, so a visible joint does not always have its own independent control.

The retargeter maps each human finger control between configured open and closed actuator targets, with scaling and limit clipping. This is how a landmark measurement becomes a robot command; it is not a direct copy of human joint angles.

One-finger testing exposed the wrong input signal

With only the robot's index finger enabled, changing my other fingers still changed its command. The robot was following the input correctly; the input itself depended too much on the surrounding hand pose. Broad open-hand/fist tests had missed this.

I rebuilt the extractor around each finger's own base knuckle, intermediate joints, and tip. Local joint angles measured curl; tip displacement along a palm-relative forward axis, divided by finger length, measured extension. The palm frame came from the wrist and index, middle, and pinky knuckles. The thumb used separate joint geometry and an opposition measure.

Extension matched the live poses more intuitively than raw curl. For the four long fingers, the final control became bend = clamp(1 − extension, 0, 1), derived after smoothing extension. Raw curl stayed visible for diagnosis; the thumb kept a conservative mapping. Synthetic single-finger tests and another live index-only trial checked that the controls were now independent.

I then connected all five fingers. Open palm, fist, and pointing worked, while pinch and peace remained approximate. This controlled the fingers, but the fixed-base hand still could not travel to an object.

Moving the whole hand

Translation and wrist rotation were harder to separate. Making a fist or turning my wrist could move the robot sideways or into the floor boundary. Left/right conventions, noisy orientation, and weak depth cues all affected the mapping. Gain changes and stronger smoothing helped individual symptoms but did not make the combined controls intuitive.

I rebuilt them around a calibrated neutral pose, captured with c. Palm-center displacement in the image controlled lateral position and height. Apparent hand size controlled depth: larger than the calibrated hand meant closer to the camera. Smoothing, a deadband, and workspace bounds made that usable, but it remained a relative depth proxy—not measured 3D position.

Wrist rotation also became relative to calibration. I tested roll before adding pitch and yaw, with axis remapping, rate limits, and smoothing. The initial 45-degree bounds restricted approach poses too much; I widened roll/yaw to 120 degrees and pitch to 110 degrees. By the end, fingers, translation, depth, and wrist rotation worked together. Recording would reveal whether those movements were reliable enough to learn from.

Phase 2

Recording movements that survive replay

Here I was still the controller. I chose a task and moved my hand; the teleop code translated that movement into robot targets. An episode was one attempt, from a saved reset to success, failure, or stopping. The aim was to turn those attempts into examples a policy could later imitate.

What gets recorded—not just a video

Each control sample stores a 27-value action: three hand-base position coordinates, four quaternion values for orientation, and 20 wrist/finger actuator targets. Alongside it are the robot's actual positions and velocities, hand landmarks and features, tracking quality, timestamps, and task/object state where available. Commands and physical motion are different: a target can change immediately while the simulated hand lags behind it.

The episode also stores the initial state, selected goal, model/configuration, and simulation timing. Named array layouts specify fields, units, and coordinate frames so later code does not guess which column means what. Replay restores the scene and feeds its saved commands back through MuJoCo; it does not move the hand by playing camera footage.

I began with 60 gesture recordings: ten each of open palm, fist, point, pinch, peace, and wave. Recording waited for calibration so setup motion was excluded. Pinch needed an extra thumb–index closure mapping because the index could curl while the thumb stayed open. All 60 recordings completed headless replay before I moved to object tasks.

Reaching and pressing: success is not the same as clean data

The reach board had three target positions. I selected a target, steered the hand to it, and saved the goal with the trajectory. A recomputed success check used saved state to verify the operator's label. Separate quality checks rejected excessive tracking loss, jitter, abrupt commands, workspace saturation, and joint-limit hits.

Of 76 reach attempts, 69 reached the target, but only 55 also passed quality checks. Those were balanced across left, center, and right. Two intermediate target positions were reserved for later evaluation: reproducing a recorded target and reaching a new one are different tests.

Button press added three spring-return buttons and a target depth. The hand had to press the selected button, not merely touch any fixture. Collection eventually produced 55 clean examples across nine button/depth combinations, with intermediate depths held out. These were still narrow tasks on a fixed board.

Pushing exposed a mismatch between camera time and physics time

The first cube pushes felt delayed even when the displayed command changed promptly. Saved state showed the physical base trailing its target by centimetres. At roughly 30 camera frames per second, the loop advanced only two 2 ms physics steps per frame: 4 ms of simulated motion for about 33 ms of elapsed time.

I changed recording to 17 physics steps per control sample and saved that timing in the episode. A headless check reduced final following error from about 2.5 cm to 2 mm. More smoothing would not have fixed a simulator that was barely advancing.

A responsive hand still did not produce a useful push

After the timing fix, one attempt scooped the cube more than 10 cm into the air. The simulated palm was horizontal while my real palm was upright. I changed the reset to fingers-up, with the palm normal facing the push direction, and used camera depth motion for forward/backward travel.

Further attempts exposed a reversed reset orientation and a general control preset that silently re-enabled wrist rotation. Fixing both still left physical deflection: the support geometry crossed the tabletop, and live height changes drove the hand into it.

I disabled the interfering below-table geometry and fixed hand height and orientation. Lateral steering then spent too much time clamped at workspace limits, even on successful pushes. Since each start and target were aligned, I fixed lateral position too. Only bounded forward/backward control remained. That produced useful straight pushes in three lanes, not general cube manipulation.

The first clean batch needed another fix: replay still advanced one physics step per action instead of the recorded 17. I made it read the saved cadence. With the right reset, target, and timing, the accepted demonstrations reproduced their behaviour.

Would a better finger mapping improve the task?

I compared the original curl/bend retargeter with a palm-local fingertip mapping and an optimization-based mapping. The optimizer solved for five normalized finger controls, balancing fingertip error against actuator limits and changes from the previous solution. It used SciPy L-BFGS-B, with a projected-gradient fallback.

Across 101 push recordings and 7,176 frames, I kept the saved base motion and substituted each method's finger commands. Curl and optimization both achieved 87.1% replay success; the fingertip mapping achieved 83.2%, with overlapping bootstrap confidence intervals. Lower fingertip error did not translate into better task success. These were counterfactual replays of curl-collected trajectories, not fresh demonstrations under every method.

What the learning experiment actually had to work with

The resulting learning sets contained 55 clean reaches, 55 button presses, and 101 pushes. They covered a few targets, a small depth range, and one cube in three lanes. I defined each skill's goal parameters, preconditions, success criteria, and timeout—but no learned policy existed yet.

I froze the data with a manifest and checksums, split whole episodes rather than neighbouring frames, and reserved goal conditions for rollout tests. The old recordings had no genuine session IDs, so they could not demonstrate cross-session generalization. The next question was smaller: could a model replace my commands within these limited tasks?

Phase 3

From demonstrations to policies—and why they failed

A replay follows a fixed recording. A reusable skill needs a policy: given the robot's current state and a goal, choose the next command, observe what happened, and choose again. This phase tested whether the recorded teleop actions could teach separate reach, press, and push policies. No LLM or camera perception was involved in choosing their actions.

Learning to predict the demonstrated commands

The PyTorch loader formed examples from recorded states, goals, and actions. Inputs included robot joint positions/velocities, base pose, task-relevant object state, and numerical goal parameters. For reach, the goal identified where the hand should go; for button press, it specified the button and depth. This is what “goal-conditioned” means here—not a language prompt.

Each policy was an MLP with two 128-unit hidden layers, predicting the full 27-value action. Behaviour cloning trained it to minimize mean squared error between its normalized predictions and the recorded commands. In plain terms: in a state like this, with this goal, what did the demonstrator command?

For reach, 43 complete episodes were used for training and 12 for validation. Normalization used training data only. I trained for 100 epochs on CPU and compared predictions against unseen recorded episodes. That checks imitation of saved actions; it does not yet check whether the model can control the robot.

Letting the model drive changed the result

During rollout, a prediction sets the hand-base pose and wrist/finger targets. MuJoCo advances the physics, then the policy gets the resulting state and predicts again. Workspace and joint-limit guards stop invalid runs. Unlike offline prediction, the next state now depends on the model's own command.

The first reach model completed 17 of 35 scenarios; the other 18 stopped at workspace or joint limits. It could sometimes get to a target, but was not reliable enough to use as a skill.

I then found an evaluation mistake: I had loaded the final training epoch rather than the one with lowest validation loss. Correcting that selection, without changing the data or rollout conditions, made reach worse: none of the 35 runs succeeded. The model that best matched saved actions was not the model that controlled the hand best.

Button and push failed more abruptly. Every run stopped on a joint-limit violation at the first control step, before a meaningful press or push developed. Those zeros were not evidence of a policy trying a sensible path and missing its goal; these full-action policies could not get past the initial safety checks.

Testing the control interface before adding model complexity

Was predicting the entire hand action part of the problem? I retrained controlled variants that predicted only the base pose or only the fingers, holding omitted controls at their prior applied values. I also tested fixed-goal inputs and the broader set of 69 successful reaches, including those rejected by quality filters.

Base-only button control improved success to 33.3% and removed the joint-limit failures, but other runs now reached workspace limits. Reach and push still had no successful variants; adding the extra reach recordings did not help. The action interface mattered, but no tested simplification solved the task.

I did not add a recurrent model. These tests had not shown that the policy needed memory to disambiguate its inputs, and first-step failures did not establish that small errors were accumulating over time. Adding memory would have answered a different, still-unproven explanation.

Why this led to a different collection strategy

The result was not “imitation learning does not work.” It was that these narrow teleop datasets and full-action MLPs did not produce usable skills—even on familiar conditions. Safer, task-specific outputs were a concrete lead. Broader variation and examples of recovery were plausible data improvements, not proven causes of the failures.

I needed a dataset that covered different objects, starts, goals, and stages of a movement, with enough control information to separate intended commands from safety handling. The next phase began by trying to collect that richer data, then changed course when the webcam interface could not reliably produce the required contact movements.

Phase 4

Replacing teleop collection with scripted demonstrations

I kept the goal of learning reusable skills, but changed how I would teach them. Instead of asking a policy to copy a small set of awkward human-controlled trajectories, I would generate repeatable, physically checked demonstrations with programmatic controllers, vary the task conditions, and test a more constrained learned interface.

A shared scene—and one last teleop pilot

The new workcell contains blocks, cylinders, and pucks, return bins, an inspection pad, setup slots, and a Start button. Reach, pick, place, push, and press now share a scene: the object picked by one skill can become the held object that the next skill places.

Collecting those movements with the webcam was still awkward. The first reset confused the forearm root with the palm's control point. Low approach targets swept neighbouring objects away. I corrected the planning point, added a high transit path, restored the proper physics cadence, and tried translation-rate control with fixed wrist orientation.

Of the four retained task trials, only reach was usable. Pick/place displaced objects without grasping, push never moved its block, and press recorded no button depth. This was the practical reason for retiring live hand control from active collection. Phase 1 remained a working teleop experiment, but it was not a dependable source of these demonstrations.

How a script knows where to move

The controller receives a named task and MuJoCo's world state: object poses, the hand's pose, fixtures, contacts, and goal locations. It uses that privileged simulator information directly; it is not recognizing objects in an image yet.

For reach, the script starts at the palm/grasp point and builds waypoints: rise above the work surface, travel horizontally toward the target, enter its approach corridor, then descend. Each update moves the commanded position a bounded distance toward the next waypoint. Candidate motion is tested in a scratch copy of the simulation for collisions, limits, and disturbance before recording begins.

The command contains a base pose and finger targets. MuJoCo's mocap target is a virtual pose target attached to the dynamic hand by a weld constraint; moving it pulls the hand toward that pose. The finger targets drive the hand's actuators. This is a floating simulated hand, not an arm planning joint trajectories through inverse kinematics. Physics determines the resulting contacts and motion.

Each contact task needs different movement rules

SkillHow the movement is generated
PressApproach the named button, move along its surface normal with fixed hand posture, hold the required depth, then retract until release.
PushCompute the planar direction from object to goal, choose a contact pose using object size and hand geometry, then advance along that direction at fixed height and finger posture.
PickUse an object-family grasp offset and wrist pose, descend to contact, gradually close the fingers between configured open/closed targets, then lift and stabilize.
PlaceTransport the held object above its target, descend using the grasp/object offset, open the fingers, wait for settling, and retract.

These are state machines with contact and success checks, not just timed animations. A pick must lift the correct object at least 4 cm off the table and hold it stably through contact with multiple hand bodies. A push must leave the object supported and settled near its goal; tipping it through the target does not count.

The first grasp lifted a block but let it rotate about 24 degrees, making placement unreliable. I changed to a side-on grasp and added an explicit rotation-only hold during lift and transport. Translation and release still follow physics, but the orientation hold is a simulator-assisted constraint, not learned dexterity. Round objects also needed task-local contact/friction settings.

How those movements become training episodes

For each seeded reset, the script reads state, emits a command, advances physics, and records the command alongside the resulting robot/object state. The loop uses the same 17 physics steps per control sample established earlier. Metadata preserves the object geometry, initial poses, goal, controller settings, and timing so the movement can be reconstructed.

The new schema distinguishes requested, commanded, and applied actions, previous actions, and safety adjustments. In accepted scripted runs these commands agree and the adjustment masks are zero; the separate fields matter when a controller is clipped or rejected. The recorder also stores movement stages such as acquire, lift, transport, and release. A complete pick/place episode can yield reach, pick, and place training segments without being counted as three independent demonstrations.

The first core collection supplied 20 reaches, 20 pushes, and 20 presses. I then collected 42 complete pick/place episodes. Acceptance required replay and recomputation of the physical result—not just the script reaching its final state.

Larger blocks, taller cylinders, and heavier pucks exposed grasp and placement errors again. Correcting object-specific offsets helped. Replay also had to apply the orientation hold at the same internal cadence as recording. Checking a smaller learned interface before scaling collection

I tried learning button press from 20 scripted episodes. This time a small MLP predicted only task-relative dx, dy, dz; fixed posture and orientation came from a deterministic adapter. Its inputs included task geometry, button state, velocity, movement stage, and the previous applied delta.

The first tests exposed finger joints pushed beyond their limits during contact. Small margins inside the fixed finger posture removed those failures, after which the pilot completed 20/20 evaluation rollouts. Those runs informed the posture repairs, so this was development evidence, not an untouched final test.

Push still failed all 20 trials when the model predicted the whole motion delta: approach errors took the hand to workspace boundaries. I instead left the nominal path with the script and learned a bounded residual—a small correction, at most 1 mm and 0.5 mm along the contact axis. The combined controller completed 20/20 trials. That does not prove the learned correction helps; it still needs comparison with the script alone.

More variety, and another failure to investigate

A handful of successful templates is not enough training coverage. The procedural generator now varies source and goal poses, supported object geometry, mass, friction, and bounded controller settings within declared ranges. Each episode needs an independent reset, not the same trajectory copied with new metadata. Sessions and held-out conditions are assigned before generation.

The planned minimum is 992 successful episodes across the task conditions, followed by 120 separately labeled failure/correction examples. Before expanding, I compared four, eight, and sixteen training episodes per condition. A small model predicted bounded position corrections applied to recorded scripted trajectories, evaluated on a fixed validation set. This passed the collection-readiness checks, but tested corrected replays—not a policy choosing every movement from live state.

The expansion is unfinished. Training and validation generation added 490 accepted episodes; the test run then stopped on a light-puck lift failure at a right-bin goal. Repeating the identical setup failed again. The recorded audit contains 666 of the planned 992 successful episodes, and the rejected attempts remain visible. I have not changed the controller or thresholds to make that held-out case pass.

The next steps are completing and auditing the data, adding visual annotations, and training and qualifying the reusable policies. The point of scripting is to provide controlled demonstrations and a baseline, not to declare the learning problem solved. Only once skills can execute reliably should an LLM request them and interpret their results.
